Overview

This board lets you use hobby 2-6s Lipo/Li-ion batteries to power random 5v appliances, like charging stuff or powering other projects. It has a 5v output screw terminal for around 2.5A, and a USB-A port that supports 10W of charging. It also has an LED interface which tells you how charged your battery is.

Design

I wanted this board to take a 2-6s (7-25V) input in and output 5V, support USB-A charging, and have some kind of battery discharge protection. I used a 3A buck converter, the TPS54302, which is a popular footprint and has a lot of resources online. For USB charging, I used the TPS2513 ic, which pulls the D+/D- pins on the USB port to the correct voltages depending on the USB device. For logic, I used an ATtiny84 microcontroller, a low power mcu with 12 gpios, an 8 MHz internal clock, and 8KB of flash memory and 512 bytes of sram. To program the ATtiny, I broke out the SPI pins, so I could connect it to a separate Arduino board and upload code through the Arduino IDE. To detect voltage, I used a simple voltage divider that the ATtiny reads, and I also included a P mosfet to break the 5V outputs if the battery voltage gets too low. The ATtiny also controls 4 LEDs representing how full the battery is. The final board is 30x60mm and 2 layers, with the top layer for signals and power, and the bottom layer is mostly ground. In my last board, I had big problems with heat, so I used a lot of filled zones in this board to help carry current and distribute heat. I used KiCad to design the board and I got the board from OSH park, which is very nice for small boards like this. (it only cost $14 with free shipping!!)

Assembly

I used a cheap amazon hot plate and solder paste to assemble this board. I skimped out on a stencil but in the future I will probably buy stencils because manually spreading solder paste is miserable. My smallest components were 0603 sized, which was manageable with some care. In the end the soldering worked, and definitely way better than my last board. One oopsy I made was I flipped the mosfet's source and drain, so I did some rearranging and add extra wires to fix that.

Programming

The programming was nothing too crazy, it took some setup with another Arduino board and the Arduino IDE, and there's many tutorials online. The ATtiny only has 8 KB of flash memory, but my program was really small anyway and only took 1.2 KB. Another mistake I made with my board was that I forgot a pullup resistor for the ATtiny's reset pin, which could cause it to reset randomly, but I got lucky this time and it didn't cause me any problems.
